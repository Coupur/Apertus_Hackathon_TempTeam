The jobs are there. Why aren’t local workers being hired?

For nearly two decades, Laos has promoted international commercial agricultural investments as a core pillar of its national development strategy. Such investments – typically structured via land concessions – were seen as a promising way of creating jobs in rural areas. But these opportunities haven’t materialized as anticipated. A CDE study asks why – and investigates how to minimize the gap between investor and worker expectations.


Tina Hirschbuehl

Laos has raced to modernize by transforming its traditional subsistence economy into big business. Since around 2006, this has meant handing over vast tracts of land to foreign investors – many of them from neighbouring China, Thailand, and Vietnam – to run large commercial farms. While policymakers anticipated that these large-scale agribusinesses would generate sustainable rural employment, transfer technical know-how, and alleviate poverty, the reality on the ground has proven far more complex.

“Investors prefer to bring in workers from other districts or provinces – or even their own countries,” says Vong Nanhthavong, senior researcher at the CDE Country Office in Vientiane and co-author of a study on Laos’s commercial agricultural job market. “And workers – especially where they have the choice – would rather not work for the commercial agricultural investments.”

Why commercial agribusiness jobs are “unattractive” to locals

Workers with other options include residents of the coffee-growing Bolaven Plateau. To them, most commercial agricultural investment jobs are “unattractive”. Not only are most of these positions low-skilled and poorly paid, but many are also seasonal, requiring a time commitment precisely when workers are busy managing their own farms. Fixed working hours and a lack of flexibility make it nearly impossible for smallholders to balance wage labour with their own agricultural tasks. “To make it more attractive for local communities to work in commercial agribusinesses, employment arrangements should enable communities to combine wage labour with farming and other livelihoods,” says Vong Nanhthavong.

Workers also report being subjected to unfair management practices, such as arbitrary wage deductions by brokers claiming that output is “insufficient”, alongside a lack of basic safety equipment.

Finally – while it’s tempting to think that wages are the main issue, the study reveals they are not, though workers prefer being paid daily rather than monthly. Commercial agricultural investment wages are not necessarily low compared to official and average wages in Laos, but they compare unfavourably to income from farming, off-farm work, or international out-migration.

What investors say

For their part, investors report struggling to find “reliable, committed, and motivated” workers, according to the report. They cite high employee turnover, with staff often leaving after receiving training – or failing to return after taking leave. Finding and retraining new workers creates additional operating costs – prompting companies to seek workers elsewhere.  


Consequently, many commercial agricultural investments in southern Laos are staffed by workers from northern provinces like Huaphan, Luang Prabang, and Phongsaly, where livelihood options are scarcer. Technical, management, and even security guard positions are frequently filled by foreign workers.

From production to processing

The eight most heavily invested commodities in Laos are rubber, coffee, pulpwood, sugarcane, cassava, cattle, durian, and tea – and producing them requires a wide range of jobs across the production and processing chain. Fieldwork tasks – such as clearing vegetation, digging holes, weeding, and harvesting – are considered “unskilled”, requiring no training or certificates. Other jobs – from applying fertilizer and operating machinery to quality control and management – fall under the Lao government’s five-tier skills framework: basic skills, semi-skilled, expert/skilled, engineer, and supervisory.


Some crops are more labour-intensive than others. While tasks in sugarcane or cassava plantations are relatively straightforward, durian – a spiky, heavy fruit that grows on tall trees – requires up to three workers per hectare for pruning.

In general, jobs in processing facilities offer more skilled and better-paid opportunities. Rubber processing, for example, requires workers to perform tasks such as washing, cutting, loading latex into roasting machines, and packaging.

However, processing offers fewer overall jobs, as most investment projects don’t process their products in Laos. When processing does occur, it’s usually limited to semi-processing rather than finished goods. “Promoting domestic agricultural processing would therefore be a way of adding value and creating more skilled, better-paid jobs,” notes Vong Nanhthavong.

The way forward

Bridging the gap between commercial agribusiness and local development requires coordinated structural reforms. To enable diversified livelihoods, companies should design flexible work calendars and create regional labour pools. This would enable community members to combine wage labour with smallholder farming and other ways of making a living. To protect worker rights, policymakers should require labour brokers to formally register, ensure transparent contract enforcement, promote centralized job platforms, and expand social protections for domestic migrants.


Equally critical is aligning education and training with the actual demands of the agricultural sector. “Skills training needs to be more targeted,” explains Vong Nanhthavong. “Current agricultural and technical and vocational education and training programmes are often too general and do not provide enough practical, specialized skills aligned with agribusiness demand.”

Instead of offering lengthy and broad programmes, educational institutions and training should collaborate with companies to deliver targeted, task-specific courses, hands-on experience or internships, and essential soft skills. By ensuring that certification translates directly into better wages and extending development initiatives to remote regions, policymakers can transform commercial agriculture into an inclusive driver of long-term rural prosperity.
