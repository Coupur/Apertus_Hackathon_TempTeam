“Restoring land means restoring hope – by putting people at the centre”

Land degradation is often described using data, maps, and global indicators. Ahead of UNCCD COP17, happening in August 2026, Ingrid Teich, CDE/WOCAT scientist and international expert on land degradation neutrality, explains why it’s so important to communicate clearly what land degradation means for people and their lives.


Interview: Gaby Allheilig

“Restoring Land. Restoring Hope.” This is the motto of the 17th Conference of the Parties, COP17, of the United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD), which will take place from 17 to 28 August 2026 in Mongolia. What does “restoring hope” mean in this context?

The motto captures something that makes the UNCCD unique among the Rio Conventions. While all three conventions ultimately aim to improve people’s lives, the UNCCD most clearly places people and their livelihoods at the centre. Land is not just soil or a production factor, it’s an inherently holistic concept: Land is our home, it’s where we grow our food and where we preserve our culture and heritage. Healthy Land is linked to biodiversity conservation, water regulation, and resilient communities. When we restore land, we restore the hope in opportunities, well-being, security, and dignity.

______________________________________________________________________________

“Restoring hope is not just optimism; it’s confidence built on results”

______________________________________________________________________________

On a personal level, the word hope makes me reflect on our current moment in history, where misinformation, negative news, and a lot of marketing can make us cynical. Restoring hope means showing that positive change is possible, especially for the youth. Every hectare restored, every farmer whose land becomes productive again, every community that grows more resilient is a reminder that our actions matter. Restoring hope is not just optimism; it’s confidence built on real results. I believe that is what the UNCCD is striving to achieve.

You have worked with the WOCAT team to map and measure land degradation in many countries over the past years. What can be done to ensure that monitoring goes beyond just measuring the extent of degradation, to also capture its quality, including biodiversity, soil fertility, and water cycles?

This is one of the biggest challenges in land degradation monitoring. Just like land, land degradation, too, is a holistic concept. It has many different causes and impacts – and whether land is considered degraded often depends on how people perceive it and what they expect from it.

_______________________________________________________

“Land degradation is a process, not a snapshot”

_______________________________________________________

In addition, land degradation has both on-site and off-site effects. Degradation in mountain areas, for example, can seriously affect downstream regions. Also, land degradation is a process, not a snapshot. You need to observe changes over time and combine multiple sources of information to assess land condition. That’s why it’s so important to develop monitoring systems together with local and national experts and land users, and to tailor them to their specific context. By combining Earth observations with local knowledge and field evidence, we can build monitoring systems that are scientifically robust, locally relevant, and capable of capturing the many dimensions of land degradation and restoration.

The causes of land degradation vary greatly across regions: In the Sahel, they include overuse, climate change, and population growth; in South America, deforestation, large-scale monoculture and cattle farming; and in Central Europe, soil sealing is a key driver. How can such different realities be compared in a meaningful way?

The diversity of drivers is one of the reasons why there’s no one-size-fits-all approach to monitoring land degradation. We can’t expect a single indicator or a unified and fully standardized global method to produce globally meaningful results at the same time.

__________________________________________________________________________

“The challenge is to find a balance between standardization and meaningfulness”

__________________________________________________________________________

So, the challenge is to find a balance between standardization and comparability on the one hand, and representativeness and meaningfulness on the other. There are indicators that can be calculated in different ways but ultimately produce comparable outputs – for example, the proportion of degraded land relative to total land area, which is Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) indicator 15.3.1.

Then why is comparability so important?

This is precisely where land degradation monitoring has struggled in the past. Of course, global estimates of land degradation are important, we need to be able to identify hotspots and trends to inform policy and guide investment decisions. But if comparability comes at the cost of accuracy, you may end up with perfectly comparable data that simply don’t reflect reality. This may lead to poor decisions and misguided policies.

And what are you doing about it?

CDE and WOCAT have helped to shape and change the approach so as to make assessments more relevant. In other words, we work towards ensuring that monitoring of land condition accurately reflects what is happening on the ground. Our goal is to find a shared approach that puts people at the centre: comparable information is essential, but it should never come at the expense of accuracy or of representing the realities that countries and communities are facing.

_________________________________________________________________________________________

“Respect, better knowledge, and ownership make participatory approaches so important”

_________________________________________________________________________________________

In a new project, you’re supporting countries in introducing participatory methods for measuring and monitoring land degradation, as well as for planning and implementing actions to prevent or reverse it. Why do you place such great emphasis on involving those directly affected from the very beginning of the monitoring process?

The first and most important reason, in my view, is respect. This means recognizing and respecting local knowledge and expertise. Land users, local communities, national experts, and other stakeholders have developed a deep understanding of their landscapes through years, and often generations, of experience. A participatory approach acknowledges that they’re not just beneficiaries of assessments, but the key knowledge holders whose perspectives are fundamental to understanding the condition and dynamics of the land.

The second reason is simply that participation leads to more robust and more accurate results. If you don’t integrate local knowledge and expertise, you overlook a lot of information that’s relevant for identifying degradation processes, understanding the underlying causes of change, and recognizing restoration opportunities.

Are there other reasons?

Yes. The third reason is that our work is not just about producing maps or indicators. The assessment process itself provides an opportunity to strengthen national and local capacities, foster collaboration among institutions and stakeholders, and build ownership of both the process and its results. If people are actively involved in generating the evidence, they are more likely to trust the results, use them in planning and decision-making, and support the implementation of sustainable land management practices. For me, it’s these three dimensions – respect, better knowledge, and ownership – that make participatory approaches so important.

____________________________________________________________________________________

“Monitoring land condition is not only about degradation, but also about measuring improvement”

____________________________________________________________________________________

Do countries actually want to adopt such methods?

Absolutely. Many countries recognize that there is no external “ready-made” solution from outside. They’re also fully aware that monitoring land condition is not only about degradation, but also about measuring improvement. It helps them make decisions, prioritize investments, track progress, and communicate positive results. This is why interest is high.

Following our recent global call for participation in the new project, we quickly received more than 70 applications, particularly from African countries. These were official submissions endorsed by UNCCD focal points – a clear indication of the strong demand for practical, science-based methods that can support the implementation of land degradation neutrality. What’s equally exciting is that the project isn’t simply transferring existing methods, but is actually advancing the scientific frontier, together with the countries, in areas where important methodological challenges remain unresolved.

You mentioned that more than 70 countries applied. What about the others?

It’s important to note that only countries eligible for funding through the Global Environment Facility (GEF) could apply. If we want to understand countries’ levels of interest in assessing land degradation, it’s more meaningful to look at which countries actively engage with the UNCCD process. Countries affected by desertification – or, more broadly, by land degradation in drylands – are traditionally the most committed in reporting to the UNCCD. But degraded land and land degradation neutrality apply to all countries, not just the “affected” ones.

________________________________________________________________________________________

“In our world, long-term sustainability often loses out to short-term interests”

________________________________________________________________________________________

Many wealthy countries report far less consistently than those from the Global South. What does this mean for progress on land degradation neutrality?

Achieving land degradation neutrality requires a genuinely global effort. Strengthening participation across all countries and regions would not only improve the quality and comparability of reporting but also accelerate collective learning and support more effective action to address land degradation worldwide.

Recent research suggests that over 80% of agricultural land in the EU is degraded. What would it take for Europe to act?

The findings of recent assessments highlighting the extent of soil degradation across Europe are indeed deeply concerning. The evidence is there. The data are there. The impacts are visible. The key question is not whether the problem exists, but how it translates into policy urgency and coordinated action. This applies not only to land degradation but to many other issues as well. We live in a world where long-term sustainability often loses out to short-term interests.

Another challenge is visibility. Although land is fundamental to our lives, the UNCCD receives less public attention, media coverage, and resources than the other two Rio Conventions – even though land is central to the objectives of both the climate and the biodiversity conventions. At the same time, the importance of land also means that it’s closely linked to multiple economic and political interests, which can make decision-making more complex. But delaying action ultimately increases both environmental and economic costs.

________________________________________________

“Land is tied to place, people, and rights”

________________________________________________

The better we get at measuring land degradation, the easier it could become to compensate losses in one place through restoration elsewhere. Does this increase the risk of putting additional pressure on land used by smallholder farmers and Indigenous peoples?

This is an important concern. The land degradation neutrality framework explicitly addresses this challenge with a comprehensive set of 19 guiding principles designed precisely to prevent such unintended outcomes – and securing land tenure is central among them. Offsetting measures that undermine the rights of land users, especially small-scale farmers and Indigenous peoples, would fundamentally contradict these principles. The principles also make it clear that any counterbalancing must, as far as possible, occur within the same land type. This fundamentally limits the possibility of shifting pressures onto entirely different or vulnerable land systems. Equally important is spatial coherence in implementation.

Meaning?

That offsetting cannot be arbitrarily transferred to unrelated regions or land systems. It must remain embedded within planning frameworks that reflect real biophysical and administrative units where land use decisions are made. So, you can’t offset land degradation in one country by restoring land in another. It’s not like carbon markets, where credits can be traded internationally. Land is fundamentally different because it’s tied to place, people, and rights.

You are a member of the UNCCD’s international working group on the Convention’s future strategic framework. The group is tasked with developing the key elements of a post-2030 strategic framework. Are there already indications of what the focus will be after 2030?

The group has prepared a report that identifies the key elements and sets out a vision for the strategic framework, with people at its centre. It envisions a world where healthy, sustainably managed land improves human well-being and food security, supports ecosystem services, and helps societies cope with drought and other growing pressures on land.

The report also says that the next indicator framework should better reflect both the gains and losses of natural capital. At the same time, it should give countries the flexibility to apply the framework in ways that fit their national contexts, priorities, and Indigenous and traditional knowledge, while respecting national data sovereignty. These ideas will be presented at COP17 in Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia, where governments will deliberate and ultimately decide on the direction of the framework.

__________________________________________________________________________________

“There’s a clear need for more scientists to engage with policy processes”

__________________________________________________________________________________

A large part of your work involves putting new findings and methods into practice. Why do you work at the interface between academia, international organizations, and politics?

(laughs) Because I love it. Honestly, I think a key reason is curiosity and a strong interest in systems thinking. Land degradation, sustainability and decision-making are inherently complex problems that cannot be understood from a single disciplinary or institutional perspective. Working at the interface between academia, international organizations, and policy provides the opportunity to move beyond theory and to apply science in real-world contexts, where it can directly inform decisions, strategies, and investments. In return, engagement with policy and implementation also helps to shape research questions, making them more grounded and useful.

Does academia collaborate enough with global-level policymaking?

There’s a clear need for more academic researchers to engage with policy processes – as professionals who understand both the technical aspects of science and the realities of policy and implementation. It’s challenging, of course, but contributing to more informed, evidence-based decision-making is extremely rewarding.
