Sustainability – a bewildering (buzz)word

What does sustainability mean for the Swiss economy?

How can a society conduct economic activity without undermining the very foundations of its livelihood? For Switzerland, this question is now more urgent than ever – concerning everything from corporate governance to balancing the budget. According to CDE’s Christoph Bader and Thomas Breu, key questions of sustainability should finally be placed at the centre of Switzerland’s economic and regulatory framework.


Christoph Bader, Thomas Breu*

Anyone who wants to understand what sustainability means for Switzerland’s economy – and other economies – must look back at history. Not for the sake of nostalgia, but rather because it highlights a core problem that remains unsolved to this day: that of economic activity undermining its own foundations.

As early as 1713, Saxon’s head of mining, Hans Carl von Carlowitz, wrote in “Sylvicultura oeconomica” about a fundamental dilemma that still lacked a name: mining activities were using up timber faster than forests could regrow. In response, Carlowitz called for “consistent and sustainable use”. In an irony of history, it was not sustainability but coal that eventually solved the timber problem and ushered in the age of fossil fuels – the consequences and implications of which we are still grappling with today.

A concept uniting North and South, ecology and development policy

It was only with industrialization that this lesson of forestry evolved into a societal principle. Observing post-war consumer society, geographer Carl O. Sauer urged an “ethic of prudence and moderation” that would leave future generations a “planet worth living on” – a prelude to the concept of intergenerational justice. In the 1970s, individual assessments coalesced into an “environmental problem”: The Club of Rome warned of the “Limits to Growth” in 1972, the UN convened in Stockholm, and the widely and prominently supported Swiss working group NAWU (Neue Analysen für Wachstum und Umwelt – “New Analyses for Growth and the Environment”) declared capitalism’s “growth imperative” to be irreconcilable with sustainable development.

In 1987, the Brundtland Report provided a definition that is still quoted today: Development that meets the needs of current generations without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs and choose their own way of life. The impact of the concept stemmed less from its precision than from its universality – it brought together North and South, ecology and development policy.

The UN Conference on Environment and Development (Rio 1992) firmly established sustainability in global politics, and the Millennium Development Goals (2000–2015) translated sustainability into measurable targets for the first time: eight goals, but directed almost exclusively at developing countries. The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) of the 2030 Agenda (2015–2030) corrected this: 17 goals with 169 targets, directed at all countries – including Switzerland.

Swiss Federal Council shifts to managing lowered expectations

However, the current picture is sobering: The UN’s High-Level Political Forum (HLPF) in July 2026 noted that only 15 percent of the measurable Sustainable Development Goals are on track, while 21 percent show moderate progress. With four years to go until the target date, intense discussions have already begun about the time after 2030.

While many countries in the Global South see an international framework like this as essential even beyond 2030, Switzerland outlines its own position in its 2026 Voluntary National Review: The Federal Council points to “a mixed picture” and has abandoned its aspirations for a comprehensive transformation. Instead of renewed ambition, it is now focusing on what is “achievable” – rather than shaking things up, the emphasis has shifted to managing lowered expectations.

What does this mean for the Swiss economy?

As a small, export-oriented and important-dependent economy, Switzerland’s ecological and social footprint extends far beyond its national borders. According to the International Spillover Index of the 2026 Sustainable Development Report, Switzerland ranks near the bottom – 166 out of 169 countries assessed. This is mainly due to its role as a trade and financial hub, along with domestic consumption.

At the same time, Switzerland depends on the stability of Earth’s climate and our global economic system. Climate targets, biodiversity, and sustainable economic activities are therefore not just matters for development cooperation or foreign policy. They are core issues for our own economic and regulatory framework.

Deliberate alignment of our institutions with sustainability

In this way, the arc from von Carlowitz to the 2030 Agenda is not a simple story of progress. Rather, it’s a story of one question arising time and again: How can a society prevent its current economic activities from eroding the foundations for the future? For an open economy like Switzerland, the answer is no longer just “reforestation”, but rather “shaping institutions”.

Instead of working through externally determined goals – reporting obligations, accounting, compliance – it’s time to reverse the formula for the post-SDG era: moving away from fulfilling outside requirements and towards deliberately aligning our own economic institutions with sustainability.

Corporate governance: creating value for society instead of just maximizing profits

Companies should become agents of change. Not merely by shaping sustainable value chains, but also by enshrining a social purpose into their articles of association – a purpose that goes beyond the pursuit of profit. This is where steward ownership models come into play. They anchor this purpose in the ownership structure, thus decoupling corporate control from the logic of profit maximization. Voting rights remain with the operational managers, and profits are primarily reinvested for specific purposes rather than paid out as dividends. While this does not guarantee an ecological focus per se, it does prevent growth from becoming an end in itself and ensures that value is created rather than extracted.

Internationally, well-known companies such as the German conglomerate Bosch, the Danish brewery Carlsberg, and the outdoor apparel brand Patagonia have structured themselves in this way. In Switzerland, this formal legal structure is still rare – the Basel-based “Purpose Foundation” and “generational” (formerly “Purpose Schweiz”) are working to change that.

Through the model, companies become social institutions rather than just vehicles for profit.

Fiscal policy: Switzerland’s “debt brake” constrains room for manoeuvre

Similar questions arise in the area of fiscal policy – except that here the benchmark is the entire national economy, not just individual companies. Energy and mobility transitions, climate protection and climate adaptation – including adaptation to heatwaves, for example – require long-term public investments. Switzerland’s “debt brake”, frequently portrayed as a neutral accounting mechanism, is far from neutral here. In fact, it constrains the kind of counter-cyclical and public investments required by a post-2030 agenda with stronger climate and adaptation goals, and it does so based on a false premise: that the state functions like a private household and can only spend as much as it takes in through taxes.


Yet even a basic accounting of the national economy shows something else: not the state budget, but rather the entire economy must be balanced. Understood this way, every Swiss Franc in savings (asset) is necessarily matched by a debt elsewhere; in macroeconomic terms, there is a precise balance between private holdings, national accounts, and foreign accounts. The illustration above shows something remarkable for Switzerland: The high private surpluses (purple) of the last 40 years were not made possible by Swiss national debt, but rather by the rest of the world’s current-account deficits with Switzerland (brown).

That means: The Swiss state has maintained a current account surplus for decades and is thus a net creditor. In 2024, this account surplus stood at 7.7 percent of gross domestic product (GDP) while Switzerland’s national budget was 0.6 percent of GDP. The latter (a “flow” variable) should not be confused with national debt (a “stock” variable). The country’s national debt is calculated from the aggregate total of previous years, and – at 14 percent of GDP in 2024 – is among the lowest in the world.

Unused potential for sustainable investments

Thanks to this state of affairs, Switzerland has more fiscal room for manoeuvre than practically any other country – yet scarcely puts it to use. For some time now, it has had more than enough to fund major investments in climate-resilient infrastructure, a future-proof healthcare system, decarbonized energy supplies, and transformative education.

An example drives home what is at stake. In its long-term climate strategy, the Federal Council has calculated that unchecked climate change may cost Switzerland 38 billion Francs per year after 2050. That is four percent of GDP, to which another 11 million Francs in costs per year could amass in the healthcare sector alone after 2060.

These harms do not disappear just because they have not been budgeted for. They are borne – belatedly and unplanned – by private households, insurers, and the healthcare system, amounting to a silent, creeping loss of prosperity. By contrast, if the state makes timely investments, the mirror opposite is created: a society that is better-equipped to withstand scorching hot summers, for example.

Internationally, the Global Commission on Adaptation estimates the returns on such investments at between two and ten times the upfront cost. By contrast, the Swiss national budget lists such investments as deficits. From the perspective of our economy, they are simply the creation of valuable assets that would never exist without the corresponding “debts”.

Not “debt”, but rather lack of investment is the greatest generational injustice

Critics of this perspective warn of inflation risks and stress that this logic does not apply indefinitely. But for Switzerland, a sovereign currency-issuing country with its own national bank, it is much more relevant than the debt-brake orthodoxy would suggest: Lack of investment, not “debt”, is the greatest generational injustice today.

Whether corporate governance or fiscal policy: The next stage of the sustainability agenda should be less about new lists of goals and more about the targeted adjustment of our economic institutions – one of the most important levers for sustainable development in Switzerland and globally.

*Dr. Christoph Bader is an economist and study programme leader at CDE. Prof. Dr. Thomas Breu is CDE Director. This article is an extended version of an article first published in Die Volkswirtschaft.
