Climate change and biodiversity loss are the two most pressing problems of the 21st century. The main objective of Terrestrial Ecology Group is to investigate how climate change and biodiversity loss affect the ecology of terrestrial organisms and ecosystem functioning. To achieve this broader objective, we study soil organisms and their interactions with grassland plants as our model system. We study climate change effects by studying the impacts of climate extremes, such as heat waves and extreme droughts by measuring the response and the recovery of soil organisms and plants, and how their interactions change during and after climate extremes.

News

Paper

Spring warming uniquely stabilizes grassland productivity

Spring warming can disproportionately increase the stability of grassland productivity, both within growing seasons and across years, compared with warming in other seasons or even year-round warming. Strikingly, year-round warming increased stability within seasons but not across years, highlighting that when warming occurs can matter as much as, or more than, how long warming lasts. These results underscore the need to incorporate seasonally asymmetric climate change into our understanding and prediction of long-term grassland responses to climate warming.

Congratulations!

Nicolò’s first PhD chapter out in GCB Communications

Using plant data over two years at Hasli Outdoor Mesocosm Experiment (HOME), we show that slow- and fast-growing grassland plants exhibit biomass-trait decoupling one month after extreme drought, particularly under warmed conditions. This decoupling disappears four months after drought by the end of the growing season.

Research

How do invasive species reshape our ecosystems?

A new global meta-analysis published in Science reveals that invasive species leave distinct ecological fingerprints in terrestrial ecosystems, and that these impacts change over time. Invasive plants consistently reduce native biodiversity, with stronger effects the longer they persist. Belowground, however, impacts on soil carbon and nitrogen weaken over time, suggesting partial recovery. The study also shows that traits enabling invasion do not predict ecological impact. By comparing plants, animals, and microorganisms, the research provides a new foundation for understanding the tempo and magnitude of invasion impacts worldwide.

Research

Low elevation communities are more sensitive to heat extremes

When and where can we expect stronger ecological impacts by climate extremes? In our recent study, published in Ecology Letters, we show that soil communities in lowlands are much more sensitive to heat compared to those at higher elevations, and that their ability to recover after heat events is greater in spring than in summer. These findings help us to identify which ecological settings are more sensitive to climate extremes in a warming world.

TERR Celebrates Five Years with a Group Retreat

To mark its five-year anniversary (August 2025), the TERR group came together for a retreat with current members. The retreat included a variety of social activities—potluck dinners, barbecues, hikes, and board games—that strengthened our community spirit and gave us time to connect outside the lab. As part of the retreat, we engaged in two insightful presentations: one on the use of AI in academic research, and another from a professional journal editor. The AI session highlighted both the opportunities and challenges of integrating artificial intelligence into scientific workflows, while the editor's talk offered valuable insights into the publishing process at top-tier journals in ecology and evolutionary biology. The retreat also provided a space for collective reflection. Group members shared what they value most about being part of TERR and identified areas where we can grow to foster an even more collegial, inclusive, and supportive research environment.

Research

Climate warming disrupts the interaction between plants and soil microorganisms

An international research group involving the University of Bern has discovered that soil microorganisms shift their seasonal rhythm more than plants in response to climate warming. The resulting discrepancy between plants and microorganisms could disrupt essential ecosystem processes and jeopardize their functionality.

Research

Heatwaves have stronger impacts on cold-acclimated populations

Using populations of two Collembola species reared at different temperatures, we found that acclimation to colder environments has negative fitness consequences when facing longer-lasting heatwaves. These findings help us identify which populations are more vulnerable to heat extremes of increasing duration. The project, published in Ecology and Evolution, was part of Anouk Gremion’s BSc –it’s the first BSc thesis published in our group!

Research

Extreme droughts can widen invasion windows

In a new review paper published in Trends in Plant Sciences led by Shareen, we discuss how some non-native plants can exploit the conditions created by extreme drought for their establishment and spread. We propose a conceptual framework to suggest that maladaptive or climatic conditioning of native plant communities is crucial for understanding how invasion success (or failure) of non-native plants will unfold during and after extreme droughts.

Research

Heat extremes can increase ecological debts

In a new review paper published in Trends in Ecology and Evolution, Gerard Martínez-De León and Maddy Thakur from the Terrestrial Ecology division synthesise the main mechanisms mediating population and community responses to heat extremes, as well as their associated costs. Biological mechanisms immediately buffering the impacts of heat extremes can have lagged costs –known as ‘ecological debts’- that are likely to become more prevalent as populations or communities recover after extreme events. Gerard and Maddy propose a conceptual framework to infer how responses to heat extremes unveil over time, highlighting the roles of ecological debts in predicting population and community stability in a changing world.

Research

Shareen’s first PhD chapter out in Plant & Soil

Intraspecific plant competition can be crucial in determining how plant populations recover after extreme drought. In one of her PhD projects, Shareen shows that such competition constrains post-drought recovery in Centaurea jacea (a common meadow plant) but not in a range-expanding congener plant Centaurea stoebe. Moreover, arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi weaken the recovery of C. jacea. This project was co-led by Shareen and Ludo, and Micha participated in it as his BSc project.
