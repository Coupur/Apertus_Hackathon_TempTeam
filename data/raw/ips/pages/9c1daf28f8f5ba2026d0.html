Understanding of how plants function, grow and develop, and complete their life cycles, has been of fundamental scientific interest for centuries. Today, basic research covers many scales that integrate physiology and ecology, from molecules to cells through to whole plants and vegetation. Important applications are to be found in areas such as agriculture, conservation and climate change.
The Institute of Plant Sciences carries out research in Molecular Plant Physiology, Chemical Ecology, Plant Ecology, and Palaeoecology. It contributes to the teaching programs in BSc Biology and to several MSc courses
The IPS has strong links to the Institute of Ecology and Evolution, the Institute of Cell Biology and the Wyss Academy for Nature.

News and Events

Current Biology

Uptake pathways for volatile perception: Plants “smell” through two routes

Plants can absorb scent compounds not only through their stomata but also through their leaf surfaces. Even when the stomata are closed – for example, at night or during dry conditions – about 20 to 30 percent of the scent molecules can enter the leaf. This allows plants to detect warning signals from infested neighboring plants and activate their defenses. The findings provide insight into how nature works and could, in the long term, contribute to more targeted plant protection using fewer pesticides.

Hao Yu, Tristan M. Cofer, Heike Lindner, Lei Wang, Antonio Aristides Pereira Gomes Filho, Sicong Zhou, Michael T. Raissig, Jamie M. Waterman, Christelle A.M. Robert, Matthias Erb

Nature Climate Change

Tree hydraulic traits drive opposite range shifts in mountains as the climate warms

As climate change accelerates, montane trees are shifting their ranges, but not all in the same direction. We reveal that hydraulic traits are the key driver: species sensitive to water stress move upslope to cooler heights, while drought-resistant ones expand downslope. By analyzing over 121,000 trees across the globe, we discovered that these water-transport mechanisms predict how forests will reorganize. Understanding these traits is essential for forecasting the future of mountain ecosystems.

Ruben Manzanedo

Nature Ecology & Evolution

Aridity amplifies biodiversity effects during climate extremes in grasslands, but not in forests

Biodiversity can buffer ecosystems against climate extremes—but not everywhere equally. A synthesis of 75 grassland and forest experiments shows that plant diversity most strongly boosts productivity during extreme drought in arid grasslands through enhanced species complementarity. In forests and under heat extremes, diversity effects are far weaker, highlighting where biodiversity is most critical for sustaining ecosystem functioning under climate change.

Eric Allan, Caroline Daniel, Seraina Cappelli

Nature Communications

Alpine biodiversity varies by mountain and species worldwide

Alpine biodiversity hotspots are concentrated in the Neotropics, while most temperate mountain regions are biodiversity coldspots. Drawing on data from 32 mountain ranges worldwide, this study reveals that alpine species richness does not follow the familiar latitudinal gradient. Instead, regional history, landscape structure and biogeographical processes may hold the key to understanding global patterns of mountain biodiversity and informing conservation.

Davnah Urbach, Mark Snethlage

Nature Communications

How species networks support multiple ecosystem functions

Biodiversity sustains healthy ecosystems, but understanding how remains a challenge. Using a long-term forest biodiversity experiment in China, we show that ecosystem multifunctionality depends not only on how many species are present, but also on how species interact across trophic levels. We integrate 11 interaction networks with 34 ecosystem functions, and find that network properties strongly shape ecosystem functioning, revealing mechanisms linking biodiversity to ecosystem multifunctionality.

Gemma Rutten

The Plant Cell

The developing leaf of the wild grass Brachypodium distachyon at single-cell resolution

How do grass leaves develop into highly efficient photosynthetic organs? Using single-cell RNA sequencing, we mapped leaf development in Brachypodium distachyon from stem cells to mature tissues. The study resolves key epidermal lineages, uncovers gene networks controlling stomata formation, and identifies new regulators of cell division and cell wall modification, creating a powerful atlas for understanding leaf development and crop productivity.

Lea Berg, Paola Ruiz Duarte, Nathan Lacombe, Roxane Spiegelhalder, Heike Lindner, Michael Raissig

Nature Communications

How 7,600 years of pollen data reveal the link between land use and plant diversity in European lowlands

This study reconstructs plant diversity on the Swiss Plateau using lake sediments. It reveals that human land use has been the primary driver of biodiversity change for millennia. Diversity peaked in medieval landscapes but has recently declined due to industrial agriculture, suggesting past sustainable practices could help reverse current losses.

Fabian Rey, Willy Tinner, Erika Gobet, Eric Allan

Journal of Ecology

High climatic niche stability across Swiss tree species

How do environmental needs change as trees grow? Researchers from IPS, ETH, and the Swiss National Forest Inventory studied 43 Swiss tree species and found that young and adult trees generally occupy the same climatic niches over time. Most species showed strong niche stability, with only minor, species-specific changes. These findings suggest tree regeneration is more climatically stable—and forest populations potentially more resilient to climate change—than previously expected.

Rubén D. Manzanedo

Nature Communications

Habitat-specific trends in taxonomic, functional, and phylogenetic diversity in European plant communities over a century

A century-long analysis of 57,390 European plant community time series reveals a nuanced picture of biodiversity change. Local plant communities generally gained species, vegetation cover, functional and phylogenetic diversity, alongside increases in non-native, Red List, and generalist species. Yet trends vary strongly among habitats and disturbance histories, highlighting that biodiversity change is far from uniform across landscapes.

Bruno X. Pinho

Molecular Plant

Structure under stress: Conserved RNA structure in plant-environment interactions

Discover how RNA structures act as molecular sensors for plant stress, from temperature shifts to bacterial attacks. While conserved RNA structures offer a revolutionary path for precise gene editing and crop improvement, their evolutionary conservation in plants remains poorly understood. Current research highlights the need for rigorous, structure-aware bioinformatics and experimental validation to unlock these mechanisms for sustainable agriculture.

Dolly Mehta, Dominique Jacques-Vuarambon, Borys Alexander León Alcivar, Jingmin Hua, Chen Xiao, Lázara Aline Simões Silva, Rodrigo S. Reis

Science Advances

A succulent gene could protect crops from drought

Kalanchoë laxiflora precisely regulates CO₂ intake while minimizing water loss. Researchers at the IPS identified the stomatal transcription factor MUTE, which triggers helper cells to enable efficient water management. By understanding these genetic mechanisms, they aim to introduce succulent traits into crops like grains and vegetables. The goal are drought-resistant varieties to enhance food security and conserve water during the climate crisis.

Xin Cheng, Heike Lindner, Lidia Hoffmann, Antonio Aristides Pereira Gomes Filho, Paola Ruiz Duarte, Miro Läderach, Michael T. Raissig

Trends in Plant Science

The evolution of genetically modified organism crops

CRISPR Advantage: Unlike conventional GMOs, CRISPR-edited crops don't introduce foreign genes, making them substantially equivalent to conventionally bred plants and potentially exempt from GMO regulations. As regulatory paradigms shift, gene editing is gaining ground over traditional genetic modification, signaling a new era of sustainable and transparent food production. Nevertheless, it is important to establish clear safety standards, effective patent management, and guidelines for crop approval procedures.

Rodrigo Reis

Nature Ecology & Evolution

Growth form and lifespan of herbaceous species mediate the role of traits in short-term drought response

How do herbaceous plants survive extreme drought? Using data from 421 species across 63 grassland and shrubland sites worldwide, we show that the traits underpinning drought resistance differ among plant groups and depend on environmental conditions. While high leaf nitrogen was linked to resistance overall, root traits, plant size and trait interactions played key roles in specific groups, revealing context-dependent pathways to drought resilience.

Andreas Stampfli, Michaela Zeiter

Nature Communications

European forest carbon and biodiversity policies have a limited win-win potential

Can forests store carbon and protect biodiversity at the same time? A Europe-wide study explores how carbon stocks relate to species richness across multiple taxonomic groups. The results reveal a complex picture: biodiversity benefits often emerge when deadwood carbon is considered. These findings challenge policies focused solely on living biomass and highlight new pathways for aligning climate mitigation with biodiversity conservation.

Markus Fischer

Nature Ecology & Evolution

Plant diversity enhances ecosystem resistance to increasing grazing pressure in global drylands

Across 73 dryland sites in 25 countries, researchers examined how ecosystems withstand rising livestock grazing. Vegetation cover declined at 80% of sites, averaging a 35% loss. Plant species richness was the strongest buffer against degradation, driven by complementary traits—highlighting biodiversity as key to sustaining drylands under intensifying grazing pressure.

Hugo Saiz

Nature Plants

Root-knot nematode Meloidogyne incognita uses secondary-metabolite-mediated soil microbiome shifts to locate host plants

Plant-parasitic nematodes are among the most destructive soil-dwelling pests, posing severe threats to global agriculture. However, the interplay between plant metabolites, rhizosphere microorganisms and their potential role in guiding pathogenic nematodes to their hosts remains poorly understood. Here we explored this gap by investigating the role of benzoxazinoids (BXs), a class of defensive metabolites of maize plants, in influencing the host-seeking behaviour of root-knot nematodes (RKNs).

Lingfei Hu, Matthias Erb

SNSF Starting Grant 2025

Dissecting the anatomical requirements for CAM photosynthesis in leaf succulents

Heike Lindner has received a prestigious SNSF Starting Grant and will start her research group at the IPS in spring 2026. In the CAMatomy project, she and her team will investigate the connection between anatomical and physiological water-saving strategies that succulent plants have evolved to survive prolonged drought periods. These findings could help to engineer agriculturally important crop species for an ever-warming climate to ensure global food security.

Heike Lindner

Nature Climate Change

Why longer seasons with climate change may not increase tree growth

Longer growing seasons under climate change are often assumed to boost tree growth and carbon uptake, yet evidence across disciplines and biomes suggests otherwise. Here, we bring insights from physiology, tree-ring studies, and ecosystem research to discuss the internal and external constraints that can limit growth even when seasons lengthen. We show how variation among species and biomes raises key questions about how forests, and their functioning, will really respond to climate change.

Rubén D. Manzanedo

Quaternary Science Reviews

Climate and human forcing of montane vegetation dynamics during the past 15,600 years in the Simmental, Northern Swiss Alps

Using paleoecological data, our study investigates vegetation, fire, land-use, and environmental dynamics at an intermediate elevation site (941 m). Four major vegetation transitions were identified, with the first three driven by climate and the last—marked by the spread of Picea abies—attributed to human land use. A significant landscape opening during the Bronze Age was linked to nearby settlement activity and is unusually pronounced for this alpine region. Our findings suggest that without sustainable farming practices, future climate change may trigger substantial vegetation shifts, including a return of temperate forests.

Sina Aregger, Lieveke van Vugt, Erika Gobet, Christoph Schwörer, Matthias Erb, Willy Tinner
