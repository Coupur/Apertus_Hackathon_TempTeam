Gunter Stober Promoted to Associate Professor

The Executive Board of the University of Bern has promoted OCCR member Gunter Stober from postdoctoral researcher to associate professor, effective May 1, 2026. Gunter is an atmospheric scientist at the Institute of Applied Physics (IAP). He earned his PhD in physics from the University of Leipzig in 2009. He then spent ten years conducting research at the Leibniz Institute for Atmospheric Physics e.V. at the University of Rostock. Since 2022, he has headed the OCCR research group Atmospheric Processes and Radiometry, which is based at the IAP.

Gunter’s research focuses primarily on trace gases such as water vapor and ozone in the middle atmosphere—that is, the layer of air between approximately 10 and 120 kilometers in altitude—as well as vertical coupling driven by atmospheric dynamics.

He is currently investigating the effects of the Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha’apai volcanic eruption on the global climate. As part of the Swiss H20 Hub research consortium, he is collaborating with colleagues from EMPA, ETH Zurich, and MeteoSwiss.

The researchers are particularly interested in how the undersea eruption affects the concentration of water vapor—a greenhouse gas—in the middle atmosphere. For this purpose, the project used balloon-borne hygrometers—that is, humidity detectors—as well as a radiometer at the Zimmerwald Observatory. Radiometers measure the water vapor content in the upper layers of the atmosphere.

“The eruption was so violent that it caused a measurable increase in water vapor levels even here in Switzerland, on the other side of the planet,” Gunter says. Worldwide, the eruption propelled about 150 teragrams of water vapor into the middle atmosphere. The volcanic water vapor and the ejected aerosols led to a slight cooling of the atmosphere.

Gunter not only measures the concentration of trace gases; he also coordinates the development of new measuring instruments. He is currently working on CRYOWARA, a radiometer that, thanks to special cooling down to 50 to 60 Kelvin, is expected to deliver more precise measurement results than its predecessors. Cooling the instrument reduces electronic noise and makes it much more sensitive. 

CRYOWARA is currently being tested on the roof of the University of Bern’s Building of Exact Sciences (ExWi). It is scheduled to be installed at the Zimmerwald Observatory in the summer of 2026. In the medium term, it is expected to replace the current MIAWARA instrument there and provide continuous measurements of water vapor over Switzerland.
