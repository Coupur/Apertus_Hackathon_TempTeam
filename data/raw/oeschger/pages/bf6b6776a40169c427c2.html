Researchers gather for inaugural PAGES IMPACT workshop on past climate tipping points

Text: Leigh Martens Winiger (PAGES) / Photography: Ivan Hernandez Almeida (PAGES)

From 11–13 May 2026, researchers from around the world gathered at the University of Bern for the inaugural workshop of the new PAGES working group IMPACT (Investigating Modes of Past Abrupt Climate Change and Tipping Points).

Hosted by Prof. Frerk Pöppelmeier, head of the OCCR’s Global Biogeochemical Modelling group, the workshop marks the first official meeting of the international working group. Members of the IMPACT steering committee, representing institutions across Europe, North and South America, Asia, and Australia, were all present either in person or online.

The workshop aimed to provide a first coordinated assessment of thresholds, abrupt climate changes, and tipping points in Earth’s geological past across multiple temporal and spatial scales. As paleoclimate records provide the only direct observational evidence of large-scale Earth system tipping behavior, studying these past events will help improve constraints on future climate risks and impacts. “The ongoing global warming is substantially increasing the likelihood of crossing critical Earth system thresholds, including climate tipping points”, says Frerk Pöppelmeier. “Through the IMPACT working group, we aim to assess past abrupt changes and tipping events to better constrain uncertainties in future projections.”

A key feature of the workshop was its strongly interdisciplinary character. Participants come from a wide range of scientific backgrounds, including paleoclimatology, oceanography, climate modeling, cryosphere research, ecosystem science, geochemistry, archaeology, and social sciences. According to Frerk, climate tipping points occur across nearly all components of the Earth system, and interactions among these components can amplify destabilization through cascading effects. “An interdisciplinary approach is therefore essential to capture the complexity of these processes and to improve our understanding of their interconnected dynamics.”

The program reflected this diversity through sessions on ocean circulation, carbon cycle dynamics, terrestrial vegetation, ecosystems, cryosphere change, and human-environment interactions.

Many participants were travelling directly from the European Geosciences Union General Assembly (EGU26), making use of existing travel plans while helping to reduce additional emissions associated with international scientific meetings.

Beyond the scientific presentations, the workshop included poster sessions, breakout discussions, and focus-group activities designed to foster collaboration across disciplines and research communities.

The final day of the workshop focused on future directions and scientific impact, including a plenary discussion on how research on abrupt climate change and tipping points can contribute to programs and initiatives such as the IPCC, WCRP, and other international climate assessments. The overarching goal was to build a coordinated international framework for studying abrupt climate variability and tipping points in the Earth system — and to better understand what Earth’s past can tell us about possible future changes.
